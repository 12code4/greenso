# 01 — Vision

## Why this game

Everyone who ever dumped a bag of plastic soldiers on the carpet has already played this game in their head. The couch was a mountain range. The gap between the rug and the hardwood was a border. *Army Men: Sarge's Heroes* mattered because it took that private, universal fiction and rendered it: your little green guy, actually two inches tall, actually behind the flower pot, actually shooting across the kitchen tile.

The magic was never the shooting. The shooting was fine. The magic was **standing at the base of a step stool and understanding it as terrain.** That's the feeling we're building the entire game around.

## The fantasy, precisely

You are small, brave, and mass-produced. The world is the ordinary house you grew up in, made vast and strange by scale. War is being waged with total sincerity over territory no Giant will ever know about: the strip of counter behind the toaster, the dark country under the bed, the sovereign nation of the sandbox.

The player should oscillate between two readings constantly:

- **In-fiction:** this is a 30-meter cliff, a river, a minefield, a fortress.
- **Out-of-fiction:** that's the kitchen counter, the hose, the LEGO-strewn rug, the cereal box.

Every good moment in this game lives in the flicker between those two readings. Level design, prop language, and audio all exist to keep that flicker alive.

## Study: what Sarge's Heroes actually did right

Played and dissected as design reference (N64/PS1/PC, 1999–2000). Its load-bearing elements:

1. **Real-world levels sold the whole game.** The plastic-world military bases were forgettable; the kitchen, bathroom, and bedroom levels are what everyone remembers. Lesson: **spend the entire map budget on household spaces.** We do zero "generic military base" maps.
2. **Third-person, and the toy is the star.** You could always see your guy — the molded helmet, the rifle fused to his hands, the slightly stiff run. First-person would have deleted the fantasy. Lesson: locked third-person, camera close enough to read the plastic.
3. **The material was the violence budget.** Plastic men melt under flamethrowers, shatter, get blown apart — cartoon-legible, zero gore, weirdly more evocative than blood. Lesson: build a real melt/chip/shatter system; it's our gore, our hit feedback, and our comedy.
4. **A named cast with war-movie archetypes.** Sarge, the heroes of Bravo Company, a scenery-chewing villain. The campiness gave the campaign a spine. Lesson: small original cast, played straight.
5. **POW rescue as a signature verb.** Freeing captured comrades felt like the toy fiction (soldiers glued down / trapped) and varied mission goals. Lesson: keep rescue as a first-class objective type.
6. **Its real flaws** — camera fighting the player, fiddly aiming, empty stretches between encounters, escort tedium. Lesson list for us: modern camera + aim assist first (M0 exit criterion), encounter density over map sprawl, escorts only with an invincible, self-pathing escortee.

### What we deliberately leave behind

- The **portal/two-worlds** structure (plastic world ↔ real world). Splitting the budget between a boring world and a great world was their mistake. We are 100% real-world maps.
- **Vehicles as player verbs.** Driveable jeeps/tanks were shallow. Vehicles appear as enemies, set pieces, and scripted rides only (v1).
- **Multiplayer.** Split-screen was a period feature. Out of scope for v1; noted as future work.

## Our twist: Analog vs. Powered

Green vs. Tan alone is a re-skin. Our original angle:

> **The Tan Command stole the battery drawer.** They've allied with the modern electronic toys — RC vehicles, a robot dinosaur, motorized rail sets — and power them with the household's strategic battery reserve. The Greens are classic molded plastic: no motors, no batteries, no chance. Underdogs by manufacture.

This gives us:

- **Built-in escalation.** Early maps: Tan infantry (fair fight). Later maps: RC armor, powered factories, the Volt Throne. The tech curve is the difficulty curve.
- **A theme with teeth.** Old toys vs. new toys is nostalgia *about* nostalgia — the classic soldiers literally fighting obsolescence. Played for camp, but it hums underneath.
- **A clean MacGuffin.** Batteries are the toy world's oil. Every mission touches the battery economy: cut supply lines (M4), sink the freighter (M5), blow the factory (M7), take the throne (M8).

## Tone rules (binding)

1. Characters play it 100% straight. Nobody in-fiction knows they're toys being silly — the *comedy is ours, not theirs.* No winks at camera.
2. **The Giants:** never seen above the knee, always out of focus or off-frame, voices muffled like a trombone through a wall. Their acts arrive as weather, geology, or divine intervention (the sprinkler comes on *because it's 7 AM*, not because anyone aimed it).
3. No real-world darkness. Stakes are total within the fiction and zero outside it. The worst fate in this universe is the lost-toy fate: *under the fridge, forever.* That line is allowed to be genuinely a little haunting — that's the ceiling.
4. Era-blended household, warm and lived-in. Not a period piece, but the toy shelf skews classic. Golden-hour light wherever the sun reaches.

## Original-IP boundaries (binding)

Plastic toy soldiers as an object are a generic, public-domain toy — green and tan armies included (real bagged toy soldiers ship in these colors from dozens of makers). What we must not touch:

- **No** use of "Army Men", "Sarge's Heroes", or any 3DO/2K trademark in the title, code, store text, or marketing.
- **No** character names or likenesses from the franchise (Sarge Hawk, Plastro, Vikki, Bravo Company, etc.). Our cast above is fully original.
- **No** recreating specific protected level layouts, logos, box art, or story beats. Inspiration is structural (scale fantasy, tone, verbs), never asset-level.
- Title candidates get a trademark sanity search before anything ships publicly.

## What success looks like

Not sales — studio charter says so. Success is:

1. A stranger plays the Backyard slice (M2) and, unprompted, says some version of *"I used to do exactly this with my toys."*
2. The plastic material + melt system is distinctive enough to be recognizable in a single screenshot.
3. At least one map (aiming: Kitchen Counter or Bedroom Night) is good enough to justify the whole project on its own.
